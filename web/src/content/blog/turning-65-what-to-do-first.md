The mail starts arriving about six months before your birthday, and most of it
looks urgent. Very little of it is. Here's the order things actually happen in.

## Know your seven-month window

Your Initial Enrollment Period runs for seven months: the three months before the
month you turn 65, the month itself, and the three months after. That's the
window to enroll in Medicare without a late penalty.

Signing up in the three months *before* your birthday month is what gets your
coverage started on time. Leave it to the months afterwards and your start date
slides, which can leave you with a gap you didn't plan for.

## Work out whether you can delay

This is the question worth answering before any other, because getting it wrong
is expensive and permanent.

If you're still working at 65 and have coverage through that job, you may be able
to delay Part B without a penalty — or you may not. It depends on the coverage
and on how large the employer is. The same question applies to prescription
coverage and Part D.

There's no way to work this out from a brochure. Call someone, tell them what
coverage you have and who you work for, and get a straight answer. If you'd
rather that be me, the number's at the top of this page.

## Then, and only then, choose a road

Once you have Parts A and B, you pick one of two approaches: keep Original
Medicare and add a Supplement plus a separate drug plan, or get your benefits
through a Medicare Advantage plan instead. You can't hold both.

Which one suits you depends on your doctors, your prescriptions, how much you
travel, and what you'd rather pay monthly versus at the point of care. That's a
conversation, not a form — and it's the part I'd rather you didn't rush.

## What to have in front of you

When you're ready to talk it through, have these to hand:

- The names of every doctor you see, including specialists
- Your prescriptions, with actual names and doses
- The pharmacy you use
- Whether you have coverage through an employer, a union, or the VA

That's genuinely everything. Fifteen minutes with that list beats an afternoon of
comparing plans on your own.
